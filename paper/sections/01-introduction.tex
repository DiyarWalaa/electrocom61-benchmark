Recovering reusable components from discarded electronic equipment is done
largely by hand, which is slow and brings workers into contact with hazardous
material. Automating the recognition step is the standing motivation for
applying object detection to the problem, and systems have been built to sort
components once they have been detached from a board \citep{lu2022sorting} and
to identify them in place on the board itself \citep{sharma2024vision}. Two
constraints follow from the setting and both matter for how such a system should
be evaluated. The conveyor does not stop for the detector, so the time taken to
process one image is a deployment criterion rather than an implementation
detail. And a recycling facility runs modest hardware, so the model that can be
deployed is not necessarily the most accurate one available.

ElectroCom61 is a public dataset for this task, containing 2{,}121 images of 61
component classes carrying 12{,}937 annotations, distributed with a fixed
train/validation/test partition \citep{electrocom61}. Three published studies
report detection results on it: the dataset paper's own technical validation and
two subsequent benchmark studies \citep{yolov12paper,yolov13paper}. The figures
they report do not agree with one another: the dataset paper reports YOLOv9s at
95.9\% mAP@50, while both benchmark studies tabulate 84.9\% for the same
architecture on the same dataset. None of the three states the conditions a
reader would need in order to reconcile them --- which version of the dataset
was used, which partition the figures were computed on, what seed was drawn, or
which release of the model library produced the architectures.

Underneath that disagreement is a defect in the published partition. Fifteen of
the 61 classes have no instances in either the validation or the test set, so no
score reported on that partition can include them. They are not the rare
classes: eight of the ten most heavily annotated classes in the dataset are
among the fifteen. What determines whether a class can be evaluated is which
capture session it was photographed in, and whether that session happened to be
assigned entirely to training.

This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An audit of the released dataset, from its files alone, establishing
    that the published partition cannot evaluate more than a quarter of the
    annotation effort in the dataset, and that the cause is how capture sessions
    were allocated rather than how rare any class is.
  \item A corrected partition, released as a manifest, establishing that the
    defect is repairable without collecting new data: every class becomes
    evaluable while the split sizes and the measured test--train contamination
    are held at their published values.
  \item A controlled benchmark of five detectors on both partitions under one
    configuration, with a single documented deviation, establishing that
    correcting the split raises every model's score, and reporting mAP@50--95,
    per-class average precision, the number of classes actually evaluated and
    inference latency --- none of which the three prior studies tabulate.
\end{enumerate}

The deviation is the transformer detector's learning rate. It does not train at
the value that trains the four convolutional detectors, and the figures reported
for it use one a hundredfold lower. Section~\ref{sec:rtdetr-lr} gives the
evidence and what follows from it for comparisons across architecture families.

Two of these results run against expectation. The defect had been understating
reported performance rather than inflating it, which is the reverse of what a
broken split is normally taken to do; and the detector that records the highest
mAP@50 is not the one this application should deploy.
