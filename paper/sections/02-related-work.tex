Work bearing on this study falls into four groups: what has been reported on
ElectroCom61 itself, the application area it serves, the literature on split
construction and evaluation validity, and the reporting conventions this
benchmark adopts.

\subsection{ElectroCom61 and the results reported on it}
\label{sec:related-electrocom61}

ElectroCom61 contains 2{,}121 images of 61 electronic component classes carrying
12{,}937 annotations, photographed with several smartphones and distributed
under CC BY 4.0 with a published partition of 1478/438/205 images
\citep{electrocom61}. It is the only public dataset of its kind at this class
count, which is what makes the results reported on it worth reconciling.

Three published studies report detection results on it. The dataset paper's own
technical validation reports a headline figure of 95.9\% \citep{electrocom61};
the two benchmark studies each compare several detectors and report mAP@50
\citep{yolov12paper,yolov13paper}.

Four properties of the experimental setup are not stated by any of the three:
which version of the dataset was used, which evaluation partition the reported
figures come from, what random seed was drawn, and which release of the model
library produced the architectures. The last two are recorded in this study's
own configuration table as settings neither prior paper states.

Four quantities are reported by none of them either: mAP@50--95, per-class
average precision, the number of classes on which the evaluation was actually
computed, and any measurement of inference speed. These are properties of the
published record rather than of the models, and what follows from them is taken
up in Section~\ref{sec:disagreement}.

\subsection{Electronic component detection and e-waste sorting}
\label{sec:related-ewaste}

The application is the recovery of reusable components from discarded circuit
boards. Sorting them by hand is slow and brings workers into contact with
hazardous material, which is the standing motivation for automating the
recognition step; the dataset was assembled for that purpose
\citep{electrocom61}.

Both benchmark studies on this dataset are themselves e-waste detection systems,
and both build on the YOLO family \citep{yolov12paper,yolov13paper}. That family
dominates the work reported on ElectroCom61: every published detection result on
the dataset, including its own technical validation, comes from a YOLO variant.
Real-time operation is the reason usually given for that choice, and it is worth
being precise about the evidence: no study on ElectroCom61 reports a speed
measurement of any kind, so the preference for real-time architectures is stated
in these studies rather than demonstrated by them.

\subsection{Dataset splits, leakage and evaluation validity}
\label{sec:related-leakage}

Defects in how data is partitioned are documented across many scientific fields
and have been identified as a contributor to irreproducible machine-learning
results \citep{kapoor2023leakage}, and the ways they arise have been organised
into taxonomies covering both conventional and transfer-learning settings
\citep{apicella2025leakage}.

Group structure is the recurring mechanism. Where observations are not
independent --- repeated measurements of the same subject, samples from the same
source --- a split that ignores the grouping places related items on both sides
of the boundary and inflates measured performance; this has been shown directly
for connectome-based models \citep{rosenblatt2024leakage}, and checklists exist
for detecting the condition before a model is trained
\citep{bernett2024guiding}.

The problem has also been given tooling. Leakage-reduced splitting has been
formalised as a combinatorial optimisation over similarity between items and
solved with clustering heuristics, so that group-aware partitions can be
produced automatically rather than by hand \citep{joeres2025datasail}.

Established benchmarks have been corrected on these grounds, and the corrections
moved the numbers: on a binding-affinity benchmark, two groups independently
produced improved splits and previously reported performance fell against them
\citep{graber2025resolving,graber2025addendum}.

Every case documented in this literature is contamination --- material that
should have been held out was not, and the measured score was too high. The
complement, in which material that should have been evaluated was never
evaluated at all and the measured score is too low, is what this paper
describes, and we are not aware of it having been characterised. Its consequences
are taken up in Section~\ref{sec:generalisation}.

\subsection{Metrics and efficiency reporting}
\label{sec:related-metrics}

The COCO benchmark established mAP averaged over ten intersection-over-union
thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 as the standard detection metric, alongside the
single-threshold mAP@50 that preceded it \citep{coco}.

The complexity figures most often reported in place of a timing measurement are
known to mislead. Parameter counts and FLOPs both correlate poorly with measured
on-device latency \citep{vasu2023mobileone,chen2023run}, and on low-compute edge
hardware latency has been reported to track detection accuracy more closely than
either \citep{kong2026edge}.

Single-run results carry a similar hazard. The random seed alone moves accuracy
by a margin large enough to reorder architectures \citep{picard2021seed}, and
conclusions drawn from model comparisons with small effect sizes have been shown
to be unreliable without repeated runs \citep{gundersen2023reporting}; where
random effects during training have been studied directly, a statistically
significant difference was found to be a weak indicator of a true one
\citep{akesson2024random}.

Non-maximum suppression is a recognised postprocessing cost, and removing it is
an explicit design goal in recent detectors --- in the transformer line since
DETR \citep{detr}, and within the YOLO family in architectures trained to
produce a single prediction per object \citep{yolov10}.

None of these conventions appears in any study reported on ElectroCom61. No
study on the dataset reports mAP@50--95, a repeatability estimate, or a timing
measurement.
