Work bearing on this study falls into four groups: what has been reported on
ElectroCom61 itself, the application area it serves, the literature on split
construction and evaluation validity, and the reporting conventions this
benchmark adopts.

\subsection{ElectroCom61 and the results reported on it}
\label{sec:related-electrocom61}

ElectroCom61 contains 2{,}121 images of 61 electronic component classes carrying
12{,}937 annotations, photographed with several smartphones and distributed
under CC BY 4.0 with a published partition of 1478/438/205 images
\citep{electrocom61}.

Three published studies report detection results on it. The dataset paper's own
technical validation reports 95.9\% mAP@50 for YOLOv9s \citep{electrocom61}; the
two benchmark studies each compare several detectors and report mAP@50, both
tabulating 84.9\% for that same architecture
\citep{yolov12paper,yolov13paper}. That set was established by citation search
on Google Scholar in August 2026: of the works citing the dataset paper, two are
those benchmark studies and two cite the dataset as related work without
training on it \citep{nguyen2025iotkits,chen2025augmentation}. Every statement
below about what no study on this dataset reports is bounded by that search, in
the sense set out in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

Four properties of the experimental setup are not stated by any of the three:
which version of the dataset was used, which evaluation partition the reported
figures come from, what random seed was drawn, and which release of the model
library produced the architectures. The last two are recorded in this study's
own configuration table as settings neither prior paper states.

Three quantities are tabulated by none of them: per-class average precision, the
number of classes on which the evaluation was actually computed, and any
measurement of inference speed. A fourth, mAP@50--95, appears in both benchmark
studies only as a training-curve panel title, and in the later one additionally
as a sentence naming it with no value attached; neither tabulates a figure for
any model, so no published value of it exists. These are properties of the
published record rather than of the models, and what follows from them is
taken up in Section~\ref{sec:disagreement}.

\subsection{Electronic component detection and e-waste sorting}
\label{sec:related-ewaste}

The application is the recovery of reusable components from discarded circuit
boards. Sorting them by hand is slow and brings workers into contact with
hazardous material, which is the standing motivation for automating the
recognition step; the dataset was assembled for that purpose
\citep{electrocom61}.

Systems addressing it span the recovery pipeline. Components detached from waste
boards have been sorted automatically on a conveyor \citep{lu2022sorting}, and
identified in place on the board itself \citep{sharma2024vision}. Classification
of e-waste more broadly has been approached with customisable YOLO variants
\citep{rajeev2025ewaste} and with convolutional classifiers built for real-time
operation \citep{sarswat2024realtime}. Two problems sit either side of component
recognition: batteries must be found before disassembly, which has been done on
X-ray images of whole appliances \citep{sterkens2021xray}, and the components
must then be located precisely enough to be removed, which has been treated as a
detection problem on board images \citep{puttero2024disassembly}. The common
thread is architectural: the YOLO family predominates wherever detection rather
than classification is required.

Both benchmark studies on this dataset are themselves e-waste detection systems,
and both build on that family \citep{yolov12paper,yolov13paper}. It dominates
the work reported on ElectroCom61 in particular: in all three studies,
including the dataset paper's own technical validation, the detector is a YOLO
variant.
Real-time operation is the reason usually given for that choice, and it is worth
being precise about the evidence: none of the three reports a speed measurement
of any kind, so the preference for real-time architectures is stated in them
rather than demonstrated by them.

\subsection{Dataset splits, leakage and evaluation validity}
\label{sec:related-leakage}

Defects in how data is partitioned are documented across many scientific fields
and have been identified as a contributor to irreproducible machine-learning
results \citep{kapoor2023leakage}, and the ways they arise have been organised
into taxonomies covering both conventional and transfer-learning settings
\citep{apicella2025leakage}.

Group structure is the recurring mechanism. Where observations are not
independent --- repeated measurements of the same subject, samples from the same
source --- a split that ignores the grouping places related items on both sides
of the boundary and inflates measured performance; this has been shown directly
for connectome-based models \citep{rosenblatt2024leakage}, and, for biological
domains, checklists exist for detecting the condition before a model is trained
\citep{bernett2024guiding}. The same structure appears in object detection:
datasets built from video contain frames with high spatiotemporal correlation,
and clustering them by image feature so that whole clusters are allocated
together removes the leakage an image-level split creates
\citep{figueiredo2024leakage}.

The problem has also been given tooling. Leakage-reduced splitting has been
formalised as a combinatorial optimisation over similarity between items and
solved with clustering heuristics, so that group-aware partitions can be
produced automatically rather than by hand \citep{joeres2025datasail}.

An established benchmark has been corrected on these grounds, and the correction
moved the numbers: on a binding-affinity benchmark, previously reported
performance fell against an improved split, by margins that differed between
models \citep{graber2025resolving}.

In every case cited above the defect is contamination --- material that should
have been held out was not, and the measured score was too high. The complement,
in which material that should have been evaluated was never evaluated at all and
the measured score is too low, is what this paper describes. The possibility has
been acknowledged: a long-vocabulary detection benchmark notes that the
categories reaching its validation and test partitions may be a strict subset of
those in training, because it adopts an existing image-level partition and cannot
guarantee otherwise \citep{gupta2019lvis}. We have not found the condition
measured, its extent reported, or a cause attributed to it. That claim is of a
different kind from the ones about ElectroCom61 above: those range over three
identified studies, whereas this one is bounded by a reading of a literature
rather than by an enumerable set, and should be read as
such. Its consequences are taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:generalisation}.

\subsection{Metrics and efficiency reporting}
\label{sec:related-metrics}

The COCO benchmark established mAP averaged over ten intersection-over-union
thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 as the standard detection metric, alongside the
single-threshold mAP@50 that preceded it \citep{coco}.

The complexity figures most often reported in place of a timing measurement are
known to mislead, and they do not mislead equally. Ranking several
mobile-friendly networks deployed on a mobile device, one study finds latency
only weakly correlated with parameter count, at a Spearman coefficient of 0.30
with $p = 0.18$, and moderately correlated with FLOPs, at 0.47 with $p = 0.03$
\citep{vasu2023mobileone}. A second concerns FLOPs alone: reducing them does not
reduce latency proportionally, because latency is FLOPs divided by an achieved
operations-per-second rate that falls for memory-bound operators
\citep{chen2023run}. On low-compute edge hardware, latency has been reported to
track detection accuracy more closely than either quantity \citep{kong2026edge}.

Single-run results carry a similar hazard. Scanning many seeds on standard image
benchmarks finds the spread between them modest, but seeds performing markedly
better or worse than the average easy to come across \citep{picard2021seed}, and
conclusions drawn from model comparisons with small effect sizes have been shown
to be unreliable without repeated runs \citep{gundersen2023reporting}; where
random effects during training have been studied directly, a statistically
significant difference was found to be a weak indicator of a true one
\citep{akesson2024random}.

Non-maximum suppression is a recognised postprocessing cost, and removing it is
an explicit design goal in recent detectors --- in the transformer line since
DETR \citep{detr}, and within the YOLO family in architectures trained to
produce a single prediction per object \citep{yolov10}.

These conventions appear only partly in the three studies reported on
ElectroCom61. Both benchmark studies plot mAP@50--95 in their training-curve
figures, but neither tabulates a value for any model, so no figure from either
is available to compare against. One of the two does report variance: its
results table gives each accuracy figure as a mean and standard deviation with a
95\% confidence interval, and its text reports paired $t$-tests at $p < 0.05$. It
states neither how many runs produced those estimates nor by what procedure,
and the later study reproduces the same table without them. None of the three
reports a timing measurement.
