Two questions are asked of the ten runs. What does the corrected split change
about the measured accuracy of these detectors, and which detector should a
conveyor-belt application actually deploy? The two have different answers, and
the second is not derivable from the first.

\subsection{Accuracy on both splits}
\label{sec:accuracy}

Every model scores higher on the corrected split than on the published one, on
both accuracy metrics and on both evaluation partitions. On test, mAP@50 rises
by between 2.73 and 4.27 points and mAP@50--95 by between 1.03 and 1.88, with no
model excepted; Table~\ref{tab:main-results} gives the figures and
Figure~\ref{fig:published-vs-corrected} shows them as two panels. The uniformity
matters as much as the size: a change that raised some models and lowered others
would indicate the two splits differ in difficulty in some model-specific way,
whereas a shift in one direction for all five indicates a property of the data.

This inverts the usual expectation, and the inversion is the finding. A split
with a defect normally inflates the scores measured on it, because the usual
defect is leakage: a test image that also appears in training is easy for the
wrong reason, so repairing the split lowers the numbers. Here repairing it
raises them. The reason is that the published split's defect was not leakage but
exclusion, and what it excluded was disproportionately easy. The fifteen classes
returned to the evaluation partitions by Section~\ref{sec:corrected-split} are
measurably easier than the classes that were already there: across all five
architectures, those fifteen average between 0.681 and 0.698 AP@50--95 on test,
while the other 46 average between 0.571 and 0.610. The ordering holds for every
model without exception. Adding easy classes to an average raises it.

The gains on the strict metric are roughly half those on the lenient one, and
that ratio is informative rather than incidental. A class can be easy to find
and hard to localise precisely: mAP@50 accepts any prediction overlapping the
ground truth by half, while mAP@50--95 averages over ten thresholds up to 0.95
and so charges for the last few pixels of box placement. The rescued classes
clear the first bar comfortably and the second less so. Detecting them accounts
for most of the gain; localising them precisely does not.

The consequence for the literature is a plain one. Reported performance on
ElectroCom61 has been understated rather than overstated. Every mAP reported in
those three studies was computed over roughly 46 of the 61 classes
(Section~\ref{sec:class-coverage}), and the fifteen missing from that average
are the ones these models handle best. This is the opposite of the correction
one expects to have to make to a benchmark with a split defect, and it is worth
stating explicitly because the reflex on hearing ``the split was broken'' is to
assume the published numbers were too high.

\subsection{What the choice of metric changes}
\label{sec:metric-choice}

The two accuracy metrics disagree about whether the ranking of these models is
stable. Under mAP@50--95 the same architecture finishes first on both splits and
the same architecture finishes last on both: YOLO26s leads at 0.6317 corrected
and 0.6129 published, and YOLOv12s trails at 0.6017 and 0.5842. Under mAP@50 the
top three reorder between splits --- RT-DETR-l leads on both, but YOLO26s and
YOLOv9s exchange second and third place. A ranking that changes when the split
changes, while the models and the training configuration do not, is reporting a
property of the evaluation partition as much as a property of the model.

That stability should not be overclaimed, and the table makes the reason
visible. Between the first-placed and last-placed architectures sit three
models --- YOLO11s, YOLOv9s and RT-DETR-l --- separated on mAP@50--95 by a total
of 0.0023 on the corrected split and 0.0039 on the published one. Two of them,
YOLO11s and YOLOv9s, differ by 0.0001. These are single-seed measurements with
no variance estimate (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), and differences of that
size cannot be attributed to the architectures. They are reported here as a
group occupying the middle of the field, not as an ordering, and any use of
these results should treat them the same way.

The implication concerns method rather than models. mAP@50 is the only accuracy
metric the three prior studies tabulate, and it is the less reliable of the two
for choosing between models: it is the metric whose ranking moved. The
weakness is not visible from mAP@50 alone --- a single number gives no signal
that it is unstable --- and becomes visible only when mAP@50--95 is reported
beside it and the two disagree. That is the argument for reporting both, and it
is why this study treats a bare mAP@50 as incomplete rather than merely
old-fashioned.

\subsection{A published ordering not reproduced}
\label{sec:ordering}

YOLOv12s finishes last of the five architectures on both accuracy metrics, on
both evaluation partitions, and on both splits. It is last on validation and on
test, at mAP@50 and at mAP@50--95, in all eight combinations that
Table~\ref{tab:main-results} reports.

One prior study of this dataset reports YOLOv12s as its most balanced performer,
at 89.1\% mAP@50 \citep{yolov12paper}. That figure is close to what this study
measures for the same architecture: it sits within 0.3 points of the 0.894
mAP@50 measured here on the published split's validation partition, and further
from the 0.877 measured on that split's test partition. The magnitude is
therefore reproduced; what is not reproduced is the position.

The disagreement is reported as a measurement, and its scope is narrow. Under
one framework version, one set of hyperparameters, one seed and one machine, all
held constant across five architectures and stated in
Section~\ref{sec:training-configuration}, YOLOv12s ranks last rather than first.
The published comparison does not state which version of the dataset it used,
which split it evaluated on, or what random seed it drew --- and as
Section~\ref{sec:metadata-provenance} establishes, the dataset exists in at
least two versions and the metadata shipped with the later one describes the
earlier. Any of those could account for the difference, and
nothing here identifies which does. The claim made is only that the ordering does
not reproduce under a controlled configuration, which is a statement about what
can currently be established rather than about the prior result.

\subsection{Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}

The two fastest architectures are separated by more than the measurement can
resolve, but not by much. YOLO11s runs at 13.68~ms and YOLO26s at 14.64~ms
median end-to-end, 0.96~ms apart, against the 0.24~ms noise floor established in
Section~\ref{sec:efficiency-measurement} from the largest disagreement between
the two timed runs of any one architecture. The gap is four times the floor, so
the ordering is real; four times a floor is also a thin margin on which to
choose a model, and the two should be treated as comparable in latency and
separated on other grounds. Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} gives the per-architecture
figures.

Neither complexity measure predicts speed reliably, and the more familiar one
fails worse. Parameter count orders the five architectures in an order almost
unrelated to their latency: YOLOv9s holds the fewest parameters at 7{,}190{,}695
fused and is the second slowest of the five. GFLOPs does substantially better,
reproducing the latency order exactly apart from the leading pair, which it
transposes --- and that pair is the 0.96~ms one, four times the noise floor.
Reporting parameter counts as a proxy for deployment cost, as is common, would
give a reader the wrong ordering here; reporting GFLOPs would give very nearly
the right one.

The two architectures without non-maximum suppression carry the lowest
postprocessing cost, which is the saving an NMS-free detection head is designed
to produce \citep{yolov10}, and the size of that advantage should be stated
carefully.
YOLO26s spends 0.38~ms in postprocessing and RT-DETR-l 0.57~ms, against 1.06 to
1.08~ms for the three architectures that run NMS --- a ratio of 2.8 between the
extremes, and an absolute saving of about 0.68~ms for YOLO26s against YOLO11s.
Table~\ref{tab:latency-breakdown} gives the stage breakdown. At batch size one
that saving is modest: it is 2.6\% of YOLO26s's end-to-end time against 7.7\% of
YOLO11s's. NMS cost grows with the number of predictions processed, so the
advantage would be expected to widen with batch size, but every measurement
reported here is at batch size one and none of them establishes by how much.
That is a limit of the protocol chosen in
Section~\ref{sec:efficiency-measurement}, not a finding about the architectures.

\subsection{Accuracy is not the deployment criterion}
\label{sec:deployment}

Taking accuracy and latency together, two of the five architectures are not
dominated by any other. YOLO11s records the lowest latency of the five, and
YOLO26s the highest mAP@50--95 on both splits.
Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy-vs-latency} plots
the trade-off, with marker area carrying fused GFLOPs so that all three
quantities are visible at once.

The front is narrower than it looks, and the reason should be disclosed rather
than smoothed over. YOLOv9s is excluded from it by 0.0001 mAP@50--95 --- 0.6186
against YOLO11s's 0.6187 --- which is below the precision at which these figures
are reported and far below anything a single seed could establish. It is
excluded on latency, not on accuracy: it runs 7.37~ms slower than YOLO11s, which
is thirty times the noise floor and the one difference in this comparison that is
beyond argument. Presenting a clean two-point front without that qualification
would misrepresent how the third point missed it.

RT-DETR-l occupies a different position under each metric. It records the
highest mAP@50 on both splits, 0.9444 corrected and 0.9171 published, and does
not lead on mAP@50--95, where it places fourth of five on both. It buys that
mAP@50 lead at roughly three times the latency of the fastest architecture and
five times the fused GFLOPs of the lightest. It also required a learning rate a
hundredfold lower than the value that trains the four convolutional detectors,
for the reasons set out in Section~\ref{sec:rtdetr-lr}; the configuration under
which it is reported here is not the configuration applied to the others.

The recommendation for the application that motivates this study is YOLO26s. It
records the highest mAP@50--95 on both splits, the lowest fused GFLOPs of the
five at 20.892, and the lowest postprocessing cost at 0.38~ms, while running
within a millisecond of the fastest architecture measured. It is not the fastest
and it does not lead on mAP@50; it is the architecture that gives up least on
any of the three axes that a conveyor-belt deployment cares about.

None of the three prior studies could have reached that conclusion, for a reason
that has nothing to do with the models. None of the three measured inference
speed.
A comparison conducted on accuracy alone cannot distinguish between two
architectures that differ by 0.0001 mAP and 7.37~ms, and it has no way to
express that the difference which matters for deployment is the second one.
